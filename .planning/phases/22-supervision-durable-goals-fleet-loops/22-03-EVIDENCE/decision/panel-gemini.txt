The engineering problem requires solving two distinct but coupled challenges: revoking a claim from an unresponsive worker, and securely fencing off that worker if it is merely slow (e.g., suspended, wedged, or orphaned) and attempts to write later. The provided baseline measurements fundamentally shape the solution. First, the unmeasured kill semantics on Windows mean we cannot rely on the operating system to reliably reap descendant processes across all supported platforms; a superseded worker may very well survive and attempt a late write. Second, the suspend measurement on Linux demonstrates that relying purely on time-based liveness (like heartbeats) will trigger mass, spurious reassignments across the fleet when a suspended machine wakes up, as all workers will simultaneously breach the missed-beat threshold. Options `heartbeat-liveness` and `os-process-liveness` fail entirely to address the second half of the requirement: they offer no fencing mechanism, leaving the duplicate-execution window unbounded if a worker wakes up. Option `lease-with-fencing-token` is the only viable architecture that satisfies both requirements. By coupling a time-bounded lease with a monotonic claim epoch (the fencing token), the system shifts from attempting to guarantee process death to guaranteeing the rejection of stale effects. Even if 2 of the 6 identified fencing paths currently require review discipline rather than structural guards, the existence proof of structural guarding on the new durable Goal transitions confirms the architecture is viable in this codebase. The fencing token ensures that a superseded owner's late write is evaluated against ordering rather than time, perfectly bounding duplicate effect at zero regardless of how long the ghost worker lingers.
