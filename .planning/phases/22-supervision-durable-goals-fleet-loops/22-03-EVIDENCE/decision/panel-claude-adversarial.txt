I walked into this believing lease-with-fencing-token was obviously correct. It is
the only option that bounds duplicate EFFECT, the requirement literally asks for
"without duplicate execution or lost completion", and the suspend measurement
supports its ordering-based-refusal pro. So that is the position I will argue
AGAINST here.

ARGUMENT 1 — the fencing token defends a hazard the only measured platform says
does not occur. The Linux baseline is not ambiguous: 8 bwrap containers before
the kill and 0 after, 17 worker shells before and 0 after, and 0 DONE lines even
after waiting past the full worker sleep. An uncatchable process-group kill reaps
the entire tree. There is no superseded owner still running to fence against.
The claim-model record itself says this is "the opposite of the plan's stated
worry". lease-with-fencing-token asks us to instrument six write paths - two of
which the measurement says CANNOT be structurally guarded today - to refuse a
write that, on the measured platform, no process is alive to make. That is a real
cost paid against zero measured instances of the hazard.

ARGUMENT 2 — and this is the sharper one - a PARTIALLY fenced system is worse
than an honestly unfenced one. Four of six paths can be structurally guarded; two
cannot. Ship that and the system acquires a property it does not have: engineers
will reason "duplicate effects are refused" because the design says so, while the
heartbeat write and the workspace reservation accounting still admit them. An
unfenced heartbeat-liveness design at least forces its duplicate-effect window to
be stated out loud and detected. The failure mode of a half-closed fence is
misplaced confidence, and this program has already paid for exactly that once:
F-1 shipped because the suite proved the fixture rather than the product, and
everyone believed the coverage was real.

ARGUMENT 3 — the case for escalate is stronger than it looks, and it is not
cowardice. Success Criterion 2 says fleet claims survive kill/restart/reassignment.
The measured baseline says a killed fanout CANNOT BE RESTARTED AT ALL: orphaned
reservations exhaust the aggregate budget, nothing calls cleanup_all() at
dispatch, and a human must delete .swarm-worktrees/ by hand (F-2, open). And
workers fail as a function of elapsed run time - 4/4 at 1s but 1/4 at 10s - while
real fanout workers run for minutes (F-3, open, not root-caused). A claim model
layered over an executor that cannot restart and cannot run a worker for ten
seconds is a model whose stated windows are fiction. Choosing any mechanism now
produces a number nobody can honor.

ARGUMENT 4 — os-process-liveness just got materially cheaper and its main
objection died. Its soundness con was that "process-identifier reuse makes a
naive probe unsafe and it needs a start-time or handle binding to be sound". The
measurement answers that on BOTH hosts: /proc/<pid>/stat field 22 on Linux and
Win32 creation time on Windows, each stable across reads and distinct per
process. Combined with Argument 1's reap result, an OS probe that says "pid+start
time is gone" is a genuine liveness verdict on a local owner, not an inference.
For a single-host fleet - which is all this phase builds - that may simply be
enough, at a fraction of the fencing surface.

WHERE MY OWN ARGUMENT IS WEAKEST, stated so it can be weighed rather than hidden:
Arguments 1 and 4 both lean on the Linux reap result, and the WINDOWS KILL LEG WAS
NEVER VALIDLY RUN. Reasoning "descendants always die" from one platform is the
precise error that produced F-1, where a race Windows happened to win was read as
a race that was absent. Windows job-object teardown is a different mechanism, and
a detached descendant surviving there would resurrect the superseded-owner hazard
that Argument 1 dismisses. Argument 2 also cuts both ways: two unguardable paths
is an argument for closing four and DETECTING on the other two, not necessarily
for closing none. And Argument 3 proves the criterion cannot be CLOSED yet; it
does not prove the mechanism cannot be CHOSEN yet, and choosing it now is what
lets the ledger be built against a stated contract instead of an implicit one.

On balance, and against my own case above: the escalate argument establishes that
Success Criterion 2 cannot be closed in this state, which is true and should be
reported - but escalate is defined as "the criterion cannot be met without a
mechanism outside this plan's scope", and that is NOT what the evidence shows.
The evidence shows two open defects inside scope, owned by another agent. That
makes escalate the wrong instrument for a true observation. Between the
remaining three, only one bounds duplicate effect on a platform whose kill
behavior is still unmeasured, and unmeasured is exactly when you want the
ordering-based refusal that does not depend on the platform's process semantics
being what you assumed.
