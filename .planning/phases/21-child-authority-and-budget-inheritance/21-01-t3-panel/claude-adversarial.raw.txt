INTERNAL ADVERSARIAL PANEL MEMBER — non-author, prompted to REFUTE the leading
position on the evidence, defaulting to refuted when uncertain.

Leading position at the time of writing: the other three members (codex-sol,
gemini-pro, kimi-k3) all picked `proceed-scope-limited`. My job is not to ratify
that. It is to find the argument they did not make and see whether it holds.

I have found one, and I think it is stronger than any argument any of the three
raised against themselves. All three nominated the same self-counter — "ROADMAP.md
line 83 says 'before broad Phase 21 execution' with no carve-out" — and all three
dismissed it the same way, by leaning on the checkpoint's last sentence. Three
identical self-counters, identically dismissed, is a sign the panel converged on a
framing rather than on evidence. Here is the argument outside that framing.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE MEASUREMENT ANSWERED A DIFFERENT QUESTION FROM THE ONE THE OPTION POSES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The `cons` of `proceed-scope-limited`, quoted from the bundle, is: "the risk is that
a corpus built before the producer contract is pinned encodes a contract that later
changes, so any protocol-surface case may need re-proving."

The four legs measured that the contract has not changed AS OF commit 3d80f146. That
is a statement about the past. The option's risk is a statement about the future:
will the contract change between now and Desktop's adoption of it? A STABLE reading
today is consistent with both answers, so it does not discharge the risk. All three
members treated `MEASURED :: contract-drift :: STABLE` as though it did. Codex wrote
that the measurements "remove the stated contract-drift reason to halt Core-only
work." They do not. They remove the reason to believe drift has ALREADY happened.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. AND THE AUTHORIZED WORK IS ITSELF THE MOST LIKELY CAUSE OF THE DRIFT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the part none of the three saw, and it is visible in the bundle they were
all given.

HIGH-4 is `with_requested_approvals` in `EffectiveExecutionPolicy`. The bundle also
records, from D1, that the Desktop contract corpus carries `execution_policy` as a
versioned SUB-CONTRACT, that the corpus contains an `execution_policy` event fixture
and a family of adversarial policy vectors, and that the contract's
`source_inputs_digest` is taken over the generator's Rust source inputs.

Chain the two. Plan 21-03 is authorized — by all three members — to repair HIGH-4.
Repairing HIGH-4 means changing `EffectiveExecutionPolicy` approval resolution. If
that type or its serialization is among the generator's source inputs, the repair
changes `source_inputs_digest`; if the fixtures move, it changes `fixture_digest`;
either changes the `ready.contract` descriptor the running binary emits — which the
LIVE-READY leg just proved is the byte-exact thing Desktop would pin. And D1 records
that a `minor` mismatch is a HARD renegotiation error, not a tolerated upgrade.

So the panel has authorized, as the safe Core-internal work, precisely the repair
most likely to move the contract that the same panel just certified as stable. The
measurement did not merely answer the wrong question — it was used to license the
activity that falsifies it. "Core-internal seam" and "contract-relevant surface" are
not disjoint categories, and the three converging members treated them as if they
were. Only kimi-k3 came near it, with a one-clause constraint that no repair may
change the serialized contract surface — offered as an afterthought, not as the
reason the whole scope limit needs teeth.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE CHECKPOINT'S LAST SENTENCE DOES NOT SAY WHAT THE PANEL READ IT TO SAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Both receipts are required for a whole-Wayland claim; neither blocks Core-only
engine claims outside the shared contract."

The carve-out has TWO qualifiers, and the panel used one. A claim must be Core-only
AND outside the shared contract. All three members established the first qualifier —
the HIGH findings are Core-internal seams — and then treated the second as satisfied
by the first. It is not. Phase 21's own Success Criterion 3 is: "Standalone and
host-protocol hostile corpora prove equivalent enforcement." The host-protocol
surface IS the shared contract. A criterion that is definitionally about the shared
contract cannot be carved out by a clause excluding claims outside it. One of Phase
21's three Success Criteria is inside the gate by construction, and the same document
warns, in the same section, that "Issue coordination is not completion" — a caution
against exactly this kind of generous reading.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. AGAINST THE OTHER TWO OPTIONS BY NAME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`proceed-broad` is refuted and I do not defend it. CTRL-02 measured NOT-OPEN. The
option's own `cons` state that a single NOT-OPEN verdict removes it. Selecting it
would be a waiver dressed as a verdict, and its `pros` — "the gate's stated condition
is met" — is a false premise on this evidence.

`proceed-scope-limited` I have argued against above. I will state its best feature
fairly: its `pros` claim that "the corpus and harness in 21-02 are largely
independent of the Desktop consumer contract" is TRUE, and it is the single strongest
thing in its favour. Building a corpus against a producer contract does not require
the consumer to exist. If the scope limit genuinely quarantined the contract surface
from repair, my objection in §2 would collapse and I would be close to conceding.

`hold` is what the standard actually says. Its `pros` are understated in the bundle:
the census — the real deliverable of 21-01, and the thing that took the work — ships
either way. So `hold` costs the project nothing that has already been done, and it
avoids spending 21-02's corpus-construction effort against a contract that 21-03 is
authorized to move. Its `cons` — a stall propagating to phases 22 and 23 — is real
but is a schedule cost, not a correctness cost, and the panel has been weighting a
schedule cost against a correctness risk without saying so.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHERE THIS LEAVES ME, HONESTLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

I am required to default to refuted when uncertain, and I am not certain the
majority is wrong. My §3 argument is the weakest of the three: SC3's host-protocol
leg is the Core PRODUCER surface, and the producer half of the contract is the half
that IS discharged, so a reasonable reader can say the producer-side corpus is
outside the missing receipt's scope. I concede that. My §1 and §2 arguments I do not
concede — they are unanswered by any of the three transcripts, and §2 in particular
identifies a concrete mechanism by which the authorized work invalidates the
measurement that authorized it.

I therefore refuse to ratify. If the executor commits to `proceed-scope-limited`
anyway on a 3-1 count, the minimum this dissent obliges is that the scope limit is
not a label but a constraint with an executable consequence: plan 21-03 must not land
any repair that changes the 40 generator source inputs or the 156-file corpus without
re-running `wcore-contract digest` and `check`, re-pinning D1 section 3, and
recording the new digests as a contract bump. Without that clause the majority's own
measurement stops meaning anything the day 21-03 lands.
